How many clients is an outdated website costing you?

You know your outgoings to the last coin. The revenue you didn't get, you don't, because it appears nowhere. This article is about estimating it anyway.

Category Doing the maths By the Stratos Webdesign team Published 5 August 2026 Updated 24 October 2026

How many clients an outdated website costs you can be estimated with four numbers: monthly visitors, the share who get in touch, the share of enquiries that become clients, and what one client is worth in a year. In Stratos Webdesign's view, if you don't know these, the first step is measurement, and only then a new website.

An outdated website doesn't send you an invoice, and it doesn't warn you when something is wrong. Fewer enquiries simply arrive than could, and the difference shows up nowhere, because a client you never got leaves no trace.

There's no line in the accounts for "the one who almost wrote". It may still be the biggest item.

The calculation

It takes four numbers, and you know all four better than anyone.

  • A: how many visitors the site gets per month;
  • B: what percentage of visitors get in touch with you;
  • C: what percentage of enquiries become clients;
  • D: what one client brings in on average over a year.

Your current monthly result is A × B × C × D. Now run the same sum with B doubled. The difference between the two numbers is what you don't receive each month.

This isn't a promise that your enquiries will double. The calculation shows what it would be worth if they did, and that tells you whether it's worth pursuing at all.

If you don't know the numbers

That's the more common case, and it's a finding in itself. If you don't know how many visitors you have and how many enquiries came from them, the first task is measurement, and only then a new website. Without it, every improvement is a matter of faith.

The minimum worth seeing: where visitors come from, which page they leave on, and how many form submissions or calls the site produces.

What makes a site "outdated"?

Age on its own matters little. A fast, well-organised site built seven years ago can outperform last year's site that wants to do everything at once. These are the real signs:

  • hard to read on a phone, or it scrolls sideways;
  • it takes several seconds to load;
  • the first screen doesn't say what you do and for whom;
  • there's no clear next step, or there are five at once;
  • the price list, opening hours or service list are out of date;
  • nothing can be changed without contacting somebody.

What improves a lot with little effort?

A full rebuild isn't always the answer. These often produce the biggest change for the smallest outlay:

  • reducing image sizes: often that alone makes a noticeable difference to speed;
  • one clear button on the first screen;
  • making the phone number tappable on mobile;
  • a dedicated page for the most important service;
  • current opening hours and contact details, identical everywhere.
